Transplantation for people with HIV

People with HIV are more likely than others to develop kidney failure (Abraham et al. 2015), and once on dialysis they are less likely to receive a kidney transplant (Locke et al. 2017; Blumberg and Rogers 2019). South African surgeons showed that kidneys from deceased donors with HIV could be transplanted into recipients with HIV with good results (Muller et al. 2015). In the United States, the HIV Organ Policy Equity (HOPE) Act of 2013 made this legal, under research protocols (Doby et al. 2018). The multicenter HOPE in Action studies that followed found outcomes after kidney transplantation from donors with HIV similar to those from donors without HIV (Durand et al. 2021, 2024). After liver transplantation, a smaller pilot study found similar graft survival, rejection and HIV breakthrough, but lower one-year patient survival and more opportunistic infections and cancers with donors with HIV (Durand et al. 2022). Candidates with HIV who were willing to accept these kidneys also waited less time for a transplant (Motter et al. 2024).

Why organs from donors with HIV went unused

Use of organs from donors with HIV has been lower than expected, nationally (Boyarsky et al. 2011; Liang et al. 2024) and, before the intervention, at this center (Swaminathan et al. 2025). The center has large HIV and kidney transplant programs, and its candidates with HIV received offers from donors with HIV, but it performed only one transplant from a donor with HIV between March 2018 and November 2020, and many of the organs it declined were transplanted at other centers. Reviewing how offers had been discussed, and asking the transplant teams, identified concerns such as the risks of HIV in the donor, unfamiliarity with the outcome data, and lack of support for difficult decisions out of hours.

The intervention

In December 2020 the center introduced a multispecialty educational intervention (Swaminathan et al. 2025): teaching sessions on outcomes of transplantation from donors with HIV for infectious diseases and transplant staff and leadership; enrolling all transplant infectious diseases physicians in the offer notifications; talks by colleagues from experienced centers; and monthly multidisciplinary meetings reviewing every offer from a donor with HIV in a standard format.

What the analysis measures

The analysis compares the periods before and after the intervention. For offers, it measures how often offers were accepted, the refusal codes recorded for declined offers, and whether a declined organ was transplanted elsewhere. Because refusal codes do not record HIV status, four codes are counted as a potentially HIV-related decline. For candidates, it measures the rate of transplants per time on the waiting list, all transplants and transplants from donors with HIV. The analysis plan defines each measure.
